## The plant correspondence tradition

The classical European medical-astrology tradition assigns every plant to one or more **planetary rulers**, one or more **zodiacal signs**, and a specific position on the **qualitative axes** (Hot/Cold, Wet/Dry, often with intensity degrees). The doctrine is rich, internally varied, and grounded in centuries of empirical practice.

The foundational source for the lineage Observatory draws from is **Nicholas Culpeper's *Complete Herbal*** (1653), which catalogues hundreds of plants with their planetary rulers, qualitative degrees, virtues, and uses. Culpeper draws on earlier herbal traditions (Gerard, Parkinson, classical Galenic sources, Arabic medical-astrology) and synthesizes them with his own clinical experience.

### Three layers of plant correspondence

A plant in the classical system is described by:

**1. Planetary rulership.**
Each plant is assigned to one (sometimes more) of the seven classical planets:
- **Saturn** rules cold, dry, slow-growing, long-lived, often bitter or astringent plants. Examples: comfrey, mullein, horsetail, hemlock (toxic), yew (toxic).
- **Jupiter** rules expansive, warm, oily, generously-fruiting, often hepatic plants. Examples: dandelion, sage, oak (with reservations), milk thistle.
- **Mars** rules hot, dry, sharp, often pungent or thorny plants. Examples: nettle, garlic, ginger (heating register), pepper, aloe.
- **Sun** rules golden, central, warming, often resinous or honey-producing plants. Examples: chamomile, calendula, St. John's wort, sunflower, frankincense.
- **Venus** rules sweet, moist, flowering, often pleasure-giving or fertility-supporting plants. Examples: rose, lady's mantle, mallow, vervain (some traditions).
- **Mercury** rules variable, thin-leaved, often aromatic or nervine plants. Examples: lavender, marjoram, fennel, dill, parsley.
- **Moon** rules moist, white-flowered, watery, often lymphatic or cooling plants. Examples: cucumber, melon, lettuce, willow, cleavers, watercress.

**2. Sign correspondence.**
Plants are also assigned to specific zodiac signs by their resonant body-region and qualitative character. Plants under Cancer (Moon's sign) often work the digestive and breast region; plants under Leo (Sun's sign) often work the cardiac and central register; plants under Capricorn (Saturn's sign) often work the joints and skeletal frame; and so on.

**3. Qualitative degrees.**
The classical system scores plants on **degree of heat or cold and degree of moisture or dryness**, usually on a four-degree scale:

| Degree | Intensity |
|---|---|
| **1st degree** | Gentle, mild; suitable for ongoing use; mild constitutional support. |
| **2nd degree** | Moderate; noticeable effect; appropriate for active need. |
| **3rd degree** | Strong; significant effect; cautious use. |
| **4th degree** | Extreme; powerful effect; potential toxicity; expert use only. |

A plant is "hot in the 2nd degree, dry in the 1st" or "cold in the 3rd degree, moist in the 2nd," giving a precise qualitative profile. This is the system Culpeper uses throughout the *Complete Herbal*.

## The sympathy / antipathy principle

The classical logic for matching plants to constitutional and illness register has two complementary modes:

### Sympathy — like supports like

The **sympathetic** principle uses plants of the same planetary or qualitative register as the body or condition being supported:

- A **Saturn body** (melancholic, cold-and-dry) is **sympathetically supported** by Saturn plants in moderate doses — the constitutional register is honored and stabilized.
- A **Mars condition** (acute, hot, inflammatory) is sometimes sympathetically supported by Mars plants — the energy is given a directed expression rather than suppressed.
- A **lunar register** (rhythmic, fluid, cyclical) is sympathetically supported by Moon plants — the body's tidal nature is met with plants that resonate with it.

Sympathy works as **constitutional support** — strengthening what is constitutionally present, allowing the body's native register to operate more cleanly.

### Antipathy — opposite tempers excess

The **antipathetic** principle uses plants of the opposite planetary or qualitative register to **temper an excess**:

- A **Saturn excess** (cold, dry, depletion, melancholy) is tempered by **Sun or Jupiter** plants (warming, broadening, expansive).
- A **Mars excess** (overheating, inflammation, aggression) is tempered by **Venus or Moon** plants (cooling, moistening, calming).
- A **Jupiter excess** (over-expansion, liver overload) is tempered by **Saturn or Mercury** plants (focusing, draining, structuring).
- A **constitutional cold-dry pattern** (melancholic excess) is tempered by **hot-moist** plants (sanguine register).

Antipathy works as **excess-tempering** — pulling the body back toward balance when one register has overrun.

### Which to use, and when

The classical practitioner judgment was:
- Use **sympathy** for constitutional support and chronic ongoing care.
- Use **antipathy** for acute conditions and excess-tempering.
- Most actual prescriptions blended both — a primary plant with one principle, supporting plants with the other.

**Observatory's framing:** the Herbal Compass surfaces plants resonant with **both sympathy and antipathy** for any given constitutional or weather register, allowing the user to see both registers without prescribing one. The user and any practitioners they work with make the actual choices.

## The Herbal Compass — scope and discipline

The Herbal Compass is Observatory's plant-correspondence surface. Its scope is **strictly defined and strictly limited**.

### What the Herbal Compass does

The Herbal Compass:

1. **Surfaces plant correspondences** for a given constitutional register or body-weather signal. ("Plants resonant with Saturn weather, sympathetic register"; "plants resonant with the user's natal Mars in Cancer, antipathetic register"; etc.)
2. **Provides the classical qualitative profile** for each plant — planetary ruler, sign correspondence, hot/cold/wet/dry degree, traditional virtues as Culpeper or similar source named them.
3. **Notes the traditional uses** in symbolic register — what register the plant has historically been used to support, framed as correspondence rather than as treatment.
4. **Flags toxic, contraindicated, and intense plants** explicitly and consistently — see Cautions below.
5. **Cites the sources** for any specific attribution — particularly Culpeper, but also Cornell's *Encyclopaedia of Medical Astrology* and modern herbalist syntheses.

### What the Herbal Compass does not do

**The Herbal Compass does not:**
- **Recommend** specific plants for specific conditions.
- **Prescribe** doses, preparations, or regimens.
- **Diagnose** conditions or suggest plants based on suspected diagnoses.
- **Replace** consultation with a qualified herbalist, medical herbal practitioner, or medical doctor.
- **Substitute** for clinical treatment of any kind.
- **Make claims** about clinical efficacy beyond what the historical correspondence tradition records.

This scope limit is **non-negotiable** and is enforced throughout the module. The Compass is a **symbolic correspondence surface** that surfaces the historical-symbolic vocabulary of plant rulership; it is not a medical recommender.

### Why this scope limit matters

The classical sources — Culpeper especially — did make recommendations. *The Complete Herbal* is full of phrases like "this plant cureth the bloody flux" or "an excellent remedy for the falling sickness." That language reflects the doctrinal and clinical practice of 1653; it does not reflect modern medical standards, modern understanding of plant pharmacology, or modern safety practices.

Observatory's Herbal Compass:
- **Preserves** the historical correspondence content (planetary rulership, qualitative profile, traditional virtues as symbolic register).
- **Translates** the recommendation language into correspondence language (the plant "is traditionally associated with the Saturn register" rather than "cureth Saturnine diseases").
- **Excludes** modern claims of clinical efficacy that the correspondence tradition itself does not support.
- **Defers** all actual herbal decisions to the user in consultation with qualified practitioners.

A user who wants to **use** plants medicinally is directed to consult a qualified clinical herbalist, herbal medical practitioner, naturopath, or medical doctor. The Herbal Compass gives them **vocabulary for thinking about plant register**; it does not give them prescriptions.

## Specific cautions — toxic, contraindicated, and intense plants

The classical tradition includes many plants that modern medicine recognizes as **toxic, dangerous in certain conditions, or requiring expert handling**. These plants appear in the classical correspondence tables (often as Saturn or Mars plants in the higher degrees of heat or cold), and the Herbal Compass must handle them with **explicit and unmistakable cautions**.

### Categories of caution

**1. Toxic plants — never to be used internally without expert clinical guidance.**

Plants in this category include:
- **Hemlock (poison hemlock, *Conium maculatum*)** — Saturn, toxic; documented in Culpeper.
- **Aconite / monkshood (*Aconitum napellus*)** — Saturn or Mars depending on source; highly toxic.
- **Belladonna / deadly nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*)** — Saturn; toxic.
- **Henbane (*Hyoscyamus niger*)** — Saturn; toxic.
- **Foxglove (*Digitalis purpurea*)** — Venus or Saturn; source of digoxin; expert-clinical only.
- **Yew (*Taxus baccata*)** — Saturn; toxic.
- **Datura / jimsonweed (*Datura stramonium*)** — Saturn; toxic.
- **Pennyroyal essential oil** — Mercury; toxic.
- **Wormwood essential oil** — Mars; toxic in concentration.
- **Many alkaloid-containing plants** — case-by-case.

**Herbal Compass behavior:** these plants appear in the correspondence tables (because they are part of the historical lineage) with **prominent toxicity warnings** that cannot be dismissed and that name the plant's category as **toxic, not for internal use without expert clinical guidance**. The classical "virtues" content is documented as historical record, not as suggestion.

**2. Contraindicated in pregnancy.**

Many plants are unsafe during pregnancy due to risk of miscarriage, hormonal effects, or fetal toxicity. Examples include pennyroyal, blue cohosh, juniper berry (in high doses), tansy, rue, mugwort, sage (in concentration), and many others.

**Herbal Compass behavior:** plants known to be unsafe in pregnancy are flagged with **pregnancy contraindication warnings** in every appearance. The flag is automatic and cannot be removed.

**3. Drug-interaction risks.**

Many plants interact with prescription medications — most notoriously St. John's wort (Sun-ruled in the tradition; a potent CYP450 enzyme inducer that interacts with many drug classes), but also many others (grapefruit-family plants, gingko, garlic in concentration, hawthorn, etc.).

**Herbal Compass behavior:** plants with known significant drug-interaction risks are flagged with **interaction warnings** that direct the user to consult their prescriber or pharmacist before any use alongside medications.

**4. Intense plants — appropriate for short-term acute use only.**

Some plants are not toxic but are too intense for chronic constitutional use — they belong to acute-symptom support and become depleting or harmful with extended use. Examples include senna (eliminative; depleting if used chronically), cayenne in high concentration (Mars; intensely heating), juniper berry (Mars; diuretic, depleting), and many others.

**Herbal Compass behavior:** intense plants are flagged with **intensity warnings** that note their classical scoring (often 3rd or 4th degree heat/cold) and remind the user that this is not a constitutional-maintenance plant.

**5. Plants requiring botanical-identification care.**

Some plants have toxic look-alikes (e.g., the common parsley family includes both edible plants and the deadly hemlock; the comfrey family includes plants with hepatotoxic alkaloids); some plants vary in safety by species (e.g., not all "comfrey" is the same, not all "mugwort" the same).

**Herbal Compass behavior:** plants with identification-confusion risks are flagged with **botanical identification cautions** and direct users to professional sourcing.

### The standard caution wording

Each plant entry in the Herbal Compass includes (where applicable) a standard caution block:

> **⚠ Caution:** [Specific category: toxic / contraindicated in pregnancy / drug-interaction risk / intense use only / identification care required].
> This plant appears in the classical correspondence tradition. Its inclusion in the Compass is for **symbolic correspondence purposes**. **Observatory does not recommend internal use of this plant without consultation with a qualified clinical herbalist or medical practitioner.**

The caution wording is consistent, prominent, and not dismissible.

## The Compass and the body weather

The Herbal Compass integrates with **Body Weather** and **Natal Blueprint** to surface plants **resonant with current weather** or **the user's constitutional register**:

- **Body Weather integration:** when Saturn weather is active, the Compass can surface Saturn-ruled plants (sympathetic register) and Sun/Jupiter plants (antipathetic register) for reflection. The user sees both registers and the historical correspondence content; no recommendation is made.
- **Natal Blueprint integration:** based on the user's constitutional temperament, the Compass can surface plants traditionally associated with that constitution's support and tempering — again, both registers, no recommendation.

This is what the Compass *is* — a way to see the historical-symbolic plant vocabulary for any given constitutional or weather register. The user uses this vocabulary as they see fit, in consultation with practitioners qualified to make actual recommendations.

## Cross-tradition note (TCM materia medica)

TCM has its own extensive materia medica — the *Bencao* tradition, with thousands of substances cataloged by **nature** (hot, warm, neutral, cool, cold), **flavor** (sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, salty — each associated with one of the Five Elements), **channels entered** (which organ-channels the substance works through), and **actions** (specific clinical actions).

TCM's qualitative axes (nature and flavor) overlap conceptually with the Western Hot/Cold and qualitative-degree system but use different categories:

| Western quality | Closest TCM analogue |
|---|---|
| Hot / Cold | Nature (hot, warm, neutral, cool, cold) — direct match |
| Wet / Dry | Approximated by tonifying/draining and by specific actions (moistening, drying, draining damp, etc.) |
| Planetary rulership | No direct analogue; replaced by channels-entered and Five-Element correspondence |

**Where the systems converge:**
- **Both** treat **qualitative profile** (hot/cold, wet/dry register) as primary for matching substance to condition.
- **Both** treat **sympathy and antipathy** logic — though TCM frames it more in terms of "treat the pattern with appropriate energetic register" rather than "like-cures-like vs. opposite-tempers-excess."
- **Both** include extensive **toxicity and caution** doctrine.
- **Both** require **practitioner judgment** for actual use; the correspondence tables alone are not prescription.

**Where they diverge:**
- TCM's "channels-entered" framework has no direct Western analogue — it specifies which organ-system the substance works through, providing precision the Western planetary system doesn't replicate.
- The Western planetary-rulership system has no direct TCM analogue — TCM does not assign herbs to planets.
- Some plants have **different correspondences** in the two systems — e.g., ginger is Mars in Western tradition (hot, pungent, drying) and Lung/Spleen/Stomach-channels (warm, pungent, dispels cold) in TCM. Both readings can be useful side by side.

**The Herbal Compass and TCM:** Observatory's primary Compass framework is Western (planetary rulership, qualitative degree, sympathy/antipathy). Cross-tradition notes are included where the TCM reading meaningfully informs the symbolic correspondence — particularly for plants with strong TCM clinical use. This is a future expansion area; the initial Compass build is Western-traditional with TCM annotations where they help.

## Safety flags ⚠

- **Prescription firewall — absolute.** The Herbal Compass **never** recommends specific plants for specific conditions or prescribes doses, preparations, or regimens. The Compass is a symbolic correspondence surface, not a recommender.
- **Diagnosis firewall.** The Compass does not surface plants based on suspected diagnoses. Diagnosis is medical; correspondence is symbolic.
- **Clinical-efficacy claims firewall.** The Compass does not make modern clinical-efficacy claims for plants. Traditional virtues are documented as historical-symbolic content; modern efficacy is a different category and requires modern evidence.
- **Toxicity warnings — non-dismissible.** Toxic plants appear in the Compass with prominent, non-dismissible toxicity warnings. The classical "virtues" content for toxic plants is documented as historical record, never as suggestion.
- **Pregnancy contraindication — automatic.** Plants known to be unsafe in pregnancy are flagged automatically and consistently.
- **Drug-interaction warnings.** Plants with known significant drug-interaction risks are flagged with consistent warnings.
- **Botanical identification caution.** Plants with toxic look-alikes are flagged with identification cautions.
- **Practitioner referral.** Users interested in actual herbal use are consistently directed to consult qualified clinical herbalists, herbal medical practitioners, naturopaths, or medical doctors.
- **Self-treatment caution.** Users are reminded that self-treatment with herbs — even ones not flagged as toxic — carries real risks (drug interactions, allergic reactions, contraindications based on individual condition).
- **Classical-source caveats.** Culpeper and other classical sources made strong clinical claims by 17th-century standards; these do not translate directly to modern clinical recommendation. The Compass translates classical recommendation language into correspondence language.
