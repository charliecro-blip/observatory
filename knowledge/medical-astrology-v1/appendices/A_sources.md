# Appendix A — Source Library

## Purpose of this appendix

This appendix documents the **source library** behind the Observatory medical astrology reference. It covers:

1. The primary classical sources cited throughout the reference, with provenance and copyright status.
2. The modern synthesizing sources used for interpretation and cross-tradition framing.
3. The cross-tradition sources (TCM and, anticipated, Ayurveda).
4. Source-handling discipline — citation, fidelity, translation work.
5. Notes on **what has been translated, what has been excluded, and why** — the source-side documentation of the safety floor's translation protocol.

The reference draws on sources spanning more than two thousand years. This appendix names them and tracks the lineage.

---

## Primary classical Western medical-astrology sources

These are the foundational texts of the European medical-astrology tradition. They are cited inline throughout the reference. All are in the public domain (publication dates given for the original editions).

### Hellenistic and Late Antique period

**Ptolemy — *Tetrabiblos*** (c. 150 CE)
The foundational text of Western astrology, including substantial medical content in Book III (especially III.12-13 on body parts and diseases by sign). Synthesizes earlier Hellenistic doctrine.
- *Status:* Public domain. Multiple modern translations (Robbins, Loeb Classical Library 1940; modern Schmidt translation).
- *Used for:* Foundational doctrine of signs ruling body parts, planetary qualities, dignities, sect basics.
- *Translation discipline:* Ptolemy is generally non-fatalistic by classical standards; less translation work required than later sources. Some prognostic content excluded.

**Vettius Valens — *Anthologies*** (c. 175 CE)
Hellenistic compendium with substantial medical-astrology content.
- *Status:* Public domain. Schmidt translation (1993 onwards) is the standard English version.
- *Used for:* Hellenistic doctrine on planetary periods, dignities, length-of-life techniques (the length-of-life techniques are documented for completeness and excluded from operational use per the mortality firewall).
- *Translation discipline:* Heavy. Valens contains extensive prognostic and mortality content; excluded.

**Galen — *On the Natural Faculties*; *On the Causes of Symptoms***; **Hippocratic Corpus generally**
Underlying medical theory of the four humors, temperaments, and qualities that the medical-astrology tradition builds on.
- *Status:* Public domain.
- *Used for:* The four-humor framework, qualitative axes (Hot/Cold, Wet/Dry), classical disease theory as background context.

### Medieval and Arabic-Latin transmission period

**Abu Ma'shar (Albumasar) — *Introductorium maius*** (9th century)
Foundational text of medieval astrology with significant medical content, transmitted through Latin translation in the 12th century.
- *Status:* Public domain.
- *Used for:* Background for the Arabic-mediated lineage of medical doctrine reaching Renaissance Europe.

**al-Biruni — *The Book of Instruction in the Elements of the Art of Astrology*** (1029)
Comprehensive Persian astrological reference.
- *Status:* Public domain. Wright translation (1934) is standard English.
- *Used for:* Background; some specific doctrinal points (dignity systems, planetary correspondences).

**Guido Bonatti — *Liber Astronomiae*** (13th century)
Major medieval Latin synthesis with substantial medical-astrology content.
- *Status:* Public domain. Zoller translation (1994-2007) is standard English.
- *Used for:* Medieval synthesis of dignity doctrine and traditional medical-astrology techniques.

### Renaissance and Early Modern period — the core English-language lineage

**William Lilly — *Christian Astrology*** (1647)
The most important single English-language source for traditional astrology, including extensive horary and decumbiture medical content. Lilly is the primary reference for the 17th-century English medical-astrology lineage.
- *Status:* Public domain. Facsimile reprints widely available (Regulus 1985 edition is standard).
- *Used for:* House doctrine, dignity tables, horary technique, decumbiture frame (with extensive translation discipline on prognostic content).
- *Translation discipline:* Heavy. Lilly contains substantial life/death prognostic content, fatalistic framings of malefic placements, and extensive predictive material. The classical doctrine is preserved in technical documentation; user-facing material is translated per Part X.

**Nicholas Culpeper — *Astrological Judgement of Diseases* (Semeiotica Uranica)** (1651)
**Nicholas Culpeper — *The Complete Herbal*** (1653)
Two foundational Culpeper texts: the first is the explicit medical-astrology manual, structuring the decumbiture and disease-symbolic frame; the second is the comprehensive herbal that anchors Observatory's Herbal Compass.
- *Status:* Public domain. The herbal has been continuously in print since 1653 in various editions; the medical-astrology text is harder to find but is available in modern editions (Astrology Classics reprints).
- *Used for:* Decumbiture doctrine, plant correspondence (planetary rulership, qualitative degrees, traditional virtues), the four-party chart frame (1st patient / 6th disease / 7th physician / 10th medicine).
- *Translation discipline:* Heavy on the medical-astrology text (prognostic, life/death, and disease-naming content extensively translated or excluded). Moderate on the herbal (clinical recommendation translated into correspondence language; toxic plants preserved with non-dismissible warnings).

**Richard Saunders — *The Astrological Judgement and Practice of Physic*** (1677)
Major 17th-century English medical-astrology synthesis with explicit decumbiture and disease-prognostic content.
- *Status:* Public domain. Reprint editions available.
- *Used for:* Decumbiture practice; the critical-days doctrine; the disease-character readings.
- *Translation discipline:* Very heavy. Saunders is one of the most explicitly prognostic of the classical sources; substantial content is documented for historical completeness and excluded from operational use. The "signs of life or death" chapters are documented in Part VII as historical material and excluded from every module.

### 19th-century onward synthesis

**H.L. Cornell — *The Encyclopaedia of Medical Astrology*** (first edition 1933; revised editions through the mid-20th century)
The most comprehensive single-volume synthesis of medical astrology in English, organized as an encyclopedia of body parts, conditions, and astrological signatures.
- *Status:* Cornell's death was 1947; first edition is in the public domain in many jurisdictions; revised editions may have different status.
- *Used for:* Extended body-region attributions (especially modern extensions: lymphatic system, peripheral nervous system, adrenals), comprehensive condition-to-sign-and-planet correspondences.
- *Translation discipline:* Moderate. Cornell pre-dates much of the modern safety discourse; some pathology-naming requires translation. Cornell's body-region extensions are useful and form the basis for many of Observatory's modern-extension attributions (with the historical-extension caveat noted in the relevant entries).

## Modern synthesizing sources

These are 20th- and 21st-century practitioners whose work informs Observatory's interpretive synthesis.

**Lee Lehman — *Classical Astrology for Modern Living*** (1996) and related works
Modern revival of traditional astrology with practical clinical and constitutional emphasis.
- *Status:* In copyright; cited as scholarly source.
- *Used for:* Composite temperament method (one of the strongest modern syntheses of the eleven-factor temperament calculation), dignity-based readings, traditional doctrine in modern practice.

**Dorian Greenbaum — *Temperament: Astrology's Forgotten Key*** (2005)
The most extensive single modern treatment of temperament from the astrological tradition.
- *Status:* In copyright; cited as scholarly source.
- *Used for:* The composite temperament method's detailed factor-weighting; the planetary day/hour as constitutional signature; historical contextualization of the temperament tradition.

**Joseph Crane — *Astrological Roots: The Hellenistic Legacy*** (2007) and related works
Synthesis of Hellenistic doctrine for modern practice.
- *Used for:* Hellenistic dignity tables, sect doctrine, traditional method clarifications.

**Demetra George — *Ancient Astrology in Theory and Practice*** (2019)
Modern Hellenistic synthesis.
- *Used for:* Hellenistic background, dignity and sect doctrine, technical clarifications.

**Robert Hand — *Horoscope Symbols*** (1981); ***Whole Sign Houses: The Oldest House System*** (and various essays)
Foundational modern syntheses of traditional doctrine for contemporary practice.
- *Used for:* House-system doctrine; symbolic framework for natal interpretation; the case for Whole-Sign houses.

**Bernadette Brady — *Astrology, a Place in Chaos*** (2006); ***The Eagle and the Lark*** (1992)
Modern syntheses with attention to symbolic-not-predictive framings.
- *Used for:* Modern translation work; the move from predictive to symbolic astrology.

**Mark Jones — *Healing the Soul: Pluto, Uranus and the Twelfth House*** (2011)
Modern psychological astrology with attention to symbolic body-relevance.
- *Used for:* Modern psychological framing of the twelfth house (with the safety floor's translation discipline applied to the depth-psychology content).

## Cross-tradition sources

### TCM sources

**Giovanni Maciocia — *The Foundations of Chinese Medicine*** (1989; second edition 2005)
The standard English-language textbook of TCM for Western practitioners.
- *Status:* In copyright; cited as scholarly source.
- *Used for:* TCM theoretical framework — yin/yang, five elements, zang-fu organs, channels, pattern differentiation.

**Ted Kaptchuk — *The Web That Has No Weaver*** (first edition 1983; revised 2000)
Influential introduction to TCM for Western readers.
- *Used for:* Conceptual framing of TCM for cross-tradition work; explanation of TCM's empirical-clinical orientation.

**Lonny Jarrett — *Nourishing Destiny: The Inner Tradition of Chinese Medicine*** (1999)
Spiritual-and-constitutional dimension of TCM practice.
- *Used for:* TCM's constitutional reading framework; the inner-and-spiritual dimensions that parallel Western astrology's symbolic-not-merely-clinical character.

**Bob Flaws — *The Tao of Healthy Eating*** (1998); and various works
Practical TCM dietary and constitutional guidance.
- *Used for:* TCM dietary and lifestyle counsel relevant to cross-tradition constitutional work.

### Ayurvedic sources (future expansion)

When Ayurveda is integrated, anticipated sources include:

**Vasant Lad — *Ayurveda: The Science of Self-Healing*** (1984); ***Textbook of Ayurveda*** (various volumes)
Foundational modern Ayurvedic syntheses for Western readers.

**Robert Svoboda — *Prakriti: Your Ayurvedic Constitution*** (1989)
The single best English-language treatment of Ayurvedic constitutional reading (*prakriti*).

**David Frawley — *Ayurvedic Astrology: Self-Healing Through the Stars*** (2005)
Specifically cross-tradition between Vedic astrology (Jyotisha) and Ayurveda.

These are noted as anticipated future sources; not yet integrated.

## Source-handling discipline

Observatory's use of these sources follows specific disciplines:

### 1. Faithful citation

When a specific claim or quote is traceable to a source, the citation is given inline (short form — e.g., *Culpeper, Semeiotica Uranica*; *Cornell, Encyclopaedia*; *Lilly, Christian Astrology*; *Ptolemy, Tetrabiblos* III.12). The full source library lives here in Appendix A.

The reference does not paraphrase classical content without citation; doctrine that is traceable is traced.

### 2. The translation protocol applied to sources

Every classical source that contains content within the safety floor's exclusions is translated per the Part X protocol. The translation is documented:

- The **original source content** is preserved (in technical documentation, in Workbench access for practitioners).
- The **Observatory translation** is what reaches user-facing surfaces.
- The **rationale for translation** is documented in the relevant entry (planet, sign, house, module) and consolidated in Part X.

### 3. What has been translated — categories

The reference has applied translation to:

- **Mortality and life/death content** (Lilly, Saunders, Culpeper's *Semeiotica*, Valens) — excluded from operational use; documented in Part VII.
- **Disease-naming content** (Cornell, Culpeper, Lilly, Saunders) — translated from disease names to symbolic register language.
- **Personality-verdict content** (most classical sources) — translated from character claims to constitutional register language.
- **Fatalistic event-prediction content** (Lilly, Saunders, Bonatti) — translated from predictive to descriptive language.
- **Clinical-prescription content** (Culpeper's *Complete Herbal*) — translated from prescription to correspondence language.
- **Pathologizing framings of placements** (especially classical readings of Saturn, Mars, the 6th, 8th, 12th houses) — translated into constitutional resource-and-counterbalance language.

### 4. What has been excluded outright

The reference excludes from operational use:

- **All life/death prognostic doctrine** (Saunders chapters on "signs of life or death," Lilly's prognostic horary, Valens's length-of-life techniques, the classical decumbiture mortality content).
- **All disease-name diagnostic content from chart placements** (the move from "X placement signifies cancer / heart disease / mental illness" — all excluded).
- **All addiction-personality and mental-illness-personality framings** (older sources contain these; excluded).
- **All fatalistic-event prediction** (specific events, accidents, deaths, etc.).
- **The classical horary "decision questions" frame** for life-direction choices (when to marry, when to travel, etc., as predictive rather than reflective).

These exclusions are unconditional and not subject to any practitioner-mode setting.

### 5. Citation honesty

The reference never pretends a source said something it didn't:

- When the classical source contains fatalistic content, the source is quoted accurately and the translation is documented as Observatory's choice, not as the source's actual position.
- When sources disagree, the disagreement is preserved (per the first principles).
- When Observatory makes an interpretive move that goes beyond what any single source supports, this is explicitly framed as Observatory's synthesis rather than as inherited doctrine.
